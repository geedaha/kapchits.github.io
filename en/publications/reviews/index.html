Reviews

This page gathers information about responses to Dr. Kapchits’s books and about the work of colleagues connected with his research. Copyright in these reviews and articles belongs to their authors and publishers, so the full texts are not reproduced here: each item is given with its bibliographic details and a summary of its contents. Short quotations appear in quotation marks with the author named.

Reviews of the books

B. W. Andrzejewski

Review of: G. L. Kapchits. Somali Proverbs and Sayings in Somali and Russian with Russian Equivalents. Moscow: Nauka, 1983. 283 pp. Published in: Bulletin of the School of Oriental and African Studies, Vol. XLIX, Part 2, 1986, pp. 420–422.

The doyen of Somali studies judged the Moscow edition first of all by its scale: a corpus of more than a thousand items, which he called the largest ever published — “Kapchits’s collection, which contains over a thousand items, is the largest ever published” (B. W. Andrzejewski). The reviewer dwells on the scholarly side of the work: its critical treatment of the existing documentation, its discernment in handling oral sources, and the author’s ability to enlist the cooperation of many Somalis, among them genuine connoisseurs of their own folklore who helped with difficult points of interpretation and with cultural background. He singles out the accuracy of the Russian translations, which carefully preserve the imagery of the originals, together with the apparatus that supports them — the annotations and the glossary of Somali terms and proper names in the appendices. The one significant omission from the survey of earlier publications, and an unavoidable one, is the collection by Maxamed Shire Maxamed, dated 1974 but, as far as the reviewer could establish, available in Somali bookshops only in 1983.

The substantive criticisms are few. The note on Khadar is judged too short to be of any use to the reader: it would have been worth explaining the miraculous powers that popular belief attributes to him, allowing him to come to the rescue even in hopeless situations. Andrzejewski also questions the note in which the Somali xalaal and xaraam are equated with the Russian terms for food permitted and forbidden during Lent: the rules of conduct differ between Muslims and Christians, and the equation produces what he calls a semantic short circuit. He rates highly the analytical second part of the book, where Kapchits applies G. L. Permyakov’s structuralist paremiology to the Somali material: the framework brings order into what would otherwise remain a shapeless mass of isolated items and can serve as a heuristic device in the search for further material. The book is useful to linguists as well, since it attests archaic grammatical forms that exclude focus indicators in declarative sentences — such forms account for 7 per cent of the corpus. The review closes with the wish for an English edition, since most scholars in the field do not read Russian.

Ibraahim M. Deeq on the 1998 dictionary

“Qaamuuska Maahmaahyadu waa Qiimo Gal” — on: Qaamuuska Maahmaahyada Soomaaliyeed / The Dictionary of Somali Proverbs. Moscow: Vostochnaya Literatura, 1998. 206 pp. Published in: Somalitalk, 12 September 2002; reprinted as “Qaamuuska Maahmaahyada” in the anthology KAYDSO: Qoraalladii Warsidaha BADSO 2014–2017, pp. 418–421, where it is dated 24 April 2015.

The reviewer starts from the place of proverbs in Somali speech and from the neglect that has befallen the heritage: before the 1998 dictionary, he writes, only a small part of the proverbial stock had ever been put on record, and Kapchits’s collection of close to six thousand items became the broadest scholarly gathering of Somali proverbs in one place. He praises the quality of the printing, the introduction in which the author analyses the proverbs and explains the plan of the book, and recommends the dictionary to anyone who writes or speaks in public and wants a proverb to strengthen an argument; what strikes him most is how far a man not born Somali has gone in understanding Somali proverbs. The reprint carries the editors’ note that the piece appears with its author’s permission.

Aliqeyr M. Nur, Oslo

“The Best Book on Somali Proverbs” — on: Soomaali been ma maahmaahdo / Somalis do not lie in proverbs. Ponte Invisible, 2012. Dated 22 August 2012, Oslo, Norway.

The reviewer opens from a distance, with the thought that a nation losing its command of the word loses its civilization. The Somalis, whom Richard Burton called a nation of poets, are generously endowed with language, yet over the past few decades the Somali language and its oral literature have drifted to the margins of public attention; where the proverbial heritage is concerned, the reviewer credits the Russian scholar Georgi Kapchits with reversing that trend. A portrait of the author follows: teaching Somali language and folklore at Moscow State University and at several European universities, twenty-five years as announcer and translator in the Somali section of Moscow Radio, and, at their first meeting in Oslo in 2008, a native-like pronunciation and a speech studded with proverbs.

As for the book itself, it is described as bilingual (Somali–English) and addressed to Somali and non-Somali readers alike. In the introduction the author answers questions about the nature of the proverb, about how a proverb’s truth is to be understood, about what makes the proverbial stocks of different nations alike and what sets them apart, and about the types into which Somali proverbs fall. The first section presents 249 of the most popular proverbs and sayings, selected by a special experiment; the second offers four thousand proverbs of various types. The reviewer’s only complaint is the small font size, which nonetheless keeps the book pocket-sized and handy. His verdict: “the book of the year in regard to those written in Somali” (Aliqeyr M. Nur).

Cabdiraxmaan Faarax “Barwaaqo”

Review in Somali of: Faaliyihii la bilkeyday: Sheekaxariirooyin Soomaaliyeed / A Soothsayer Tested: Somali Folktales. Compiled and translated by G. L. Kapchits. The Way publishers, Moscow, 2006.

The reviewer recalls a conversation with Kapchits in Ottawa, in which the scholar said that young Somalis should be left something they can inherit — and, on seeing the book, concluded that this had been no empty phrase. The collection’s chief innovation, in “Barwaaqo”’s view, is that Somali narrative prose, until then an undifferentiated mass, has for the first time been sorted into genres: mythological tales (7), animal tales (17), novellistic tales (16) and didactic tales (75). One tale receives extended treatment — “Isha Cumar ka lulata”, known to some listeners as “Isha Cali ka laallaadda”: it underlies Xuseen Dhiqle’s famous poem about the lion’s share, and the reviewer quotes the poem in full.

From the same tale “Barwaaqo” draws an unexpected linguistic observation: the way the fox divides the spoils preserves an archaic vocabulary of fractions — badh, waax, falladh, rimic, miil, that is 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 and 1/32 — and many people today have no idea that Somalis knew such measures. Turning to the fourth and largest section, he stops at the tale “Gari Ilaah bay taqaan” (“Justice knows only God”) as a parable of impartial judgement, the very thing the Somali lands now lack. His conclusion is that the book is shared by children and elders alike, and that the English translation, contrary to the adage about what is lost in translation, has kept the wisdom of the tales intact: “Waa buug mudan abaal marin” — a book deserving an award (Cabdiraxmaan Faarax “Barwaaqo”) — and one he would like to see added to the school curriculum.

Maxamed Xasan “Alto”

Article in Somali on the Somali Week Festival 2011 in London and Dr. Kapchits’s participation in it. The festival ran from 22 to 30 October 2011 at Oxford House, Bethnal Green, London; the theme of that year was literary translation.

The author describes the 2011 festival as the strongest in the London series begun in 2004: the theme was literary translation, a large number of translated books were presented, and the festival worked together with the Poetry Translation Centre and its director, the poet Sarah Maguire, which prepared translations of selected poems by the invited poets. Among the guests who came from Africa, North America and Europe, “Alto” singles out Dr. Kapchits — an expert in Somali culture, folktales, proverbs and grammar, who has given more than forty years to the Somali language and worked for many years in the Somali section of Moscow Radio: “Waa ruugcaddaa looga dambeeyo afka, dhaqanka iyo hiddaha Soomaalida” — on matters of Somali language, culture and tradition he is the final authority (Maxamed Xasan “Alto”).

On the Sunday evening Kapchits read from his own writings and spoke about the wisdom, imagery and beauty of the Somali language — about the density, the allegory and the figurative texture of proverbs, sayings and folktales — and, in the author’s account, the audience received the lecture warmly. At the closing event on 30 October he addressed the gathering with an appeal to preserve the Somali language and to teach it to children growing up abroad, thanked the organisers, and ended by speaking of the peace the Somalis lack, citing proverbs about concord among brothers.

Adan Makina on the Modern Dictionary of Somali Proverbs

Two reviews of: Qaamuuska Casriga ah ee Maahmaahda Soomaaliyeed / A Modern Dictionary of Somali Proverbs. Laashin, Katrineholm, 2020. In Somali — “Aragtidayda «Qaamuuska Casriga Ah ee Maahmaahda Soomaaliyeed» ee uu Qoray Georgi Kapchits”: WardheerNews, 18 October 2020. In English — “A review of «A Modern Somali Dictionary of Proverbs» by Georgi Kapchits”: WardheerNews, 6 December 2020.

The Somali review opens with the saying “ma anaa waalan mise Cadan baa laga heesaya” — is it I who am mad, or is there really singing in Aden? — the reviewer’s way of conveying his astonishment that a scholar born in Russia should have done what he had neither heard with his ears nor seen with his eyes. Before turning to the dictionary he gives a biography of its author: birth in the Soviet Union in 1939, the Lomonosov University diploma of 1967, Radio Moscow from 1967 to 1994 and the Voice of Russia from 1994 to 2014, the doctorate in Somali grammar in 2000, teaching in Moscow and Berlin, some ten books in Somali — and calls him “walaalkeyga iga weyn”, my elder brother. The dictionary itself he presents as a legacy: five thousand proverbs gathered over decades, a safeguard for a language whose standing, in his view, has been falling since the collapse of the state in 1991; he sets it against the fate of languages that die with their last speaker. The copy in the archive is headed 17 October 2020 and names Sunday 11 October 2020 as the day Kapchits told him that the book was out.

The English review, written for WardheerNews’s English readers seven weeks later, retells the acquaintance and the biography, describes the volume — 347 pages, five thousand proverbs, published by Laashin — and turns to the reviewer’s own countrymen: educated Somalis, he writes, have neglected their native language in favour of colonial ones and bear part of the blame for its decline. He closes by urging readers to acquire the book as “a legacy” for the generations to come and calls its author “a giant all Somalis can lean on” (Adan Makina).

Until 6 September 2026 this page dated the review 28 November 2020 and listed four conflicting dates for it; the dates above are the ones WardheerNews prints on the two publications.

Peter Unseth

Review of: G. Kapchits. Qaamuuska Casriga Ah ee Maahmaahda Soomaaliyeed / A Modern Dictionary of Somali Proverbs. Laashin, 2020. 347 pp. Published in: Proverbium (Yearbook of International Proverb Scholarship), Vol. 39, 2022. Received 12 April 2021, accepted 10 August 2021.

The paremiologist Peter Unseth (Dallas International University and SIL International) opens by calling the author “the most prolific scholar studying Somali proverbs” (Peter Unseth), and describes the dictionary as a study of Somali proverbs and their structure joined to a collection of some five thousand of them. The first six chapters, twenty-five pages in all, build on the work of G. L. Permyakov: the opening chapter sets out the sign nature, themes and logico-semantic classification of Somali paremias, with its twenty-eight types of relation and its paired oppositions. The section on variants and variations reports that for the list of 249 widely known proverbs gathered by questionnaire a variant was supplied for every one, and that in a number of cases the original form is being displaced by its variant.

Then come the lists. First a series of 315 proverbs ranked by how widely Somalis recognise them — a ranking internal to this dictionary, not to be confused with the paremiological minimum of 249 yielded by the experiment. Then the main corpus, about five thousand proverbs in Somali alphabetical order, given without translation but with variants and cross-references to proverbs close in meaning. Then three thematic selections: thirty proverbs that incorporate quoted speech, twenty-seven built as question and answer, and a list of enumerative proverbs. The reviewer dwells on the last of these: of 294 enumerative proverbs, 223 — more than three quarters — are built on a list of three items, and he asks whether the preference for triads is a psycholinguistic universal or a result of contact across the Arabian Sea, comparing the Somali material with Gujarati. His one complaint is the absence of translations in the main corpus, a disappointment for the non-Somali reader, though he grants that the book is addressed to Somali readers as well.

The review also records a paper on enumerative proverbs given at the annual Interdisciplinary Colloquium on Proverbs in Tavira, Portugal, in 2018 — the latest of the author’s conference appearances for which a source exists. See Conferences & Congresses.

Adan Makina on the folktales

Review of: Isha Cumar ka lulata / The Knocked-Out Eye of the Hyena: Somali Folk Tales. Hiil Press, 2022. Published in: WardheerNews, 21 November 2022.

The reviewer works through the collection section by section — the animal tales, the mythological subjects, the cycle about Carraweelo (also called Cebla’), the two giants, Wiil-Waal (Garaad Faarax Garaad Xirsi) and Cigaal Shidaad — retelling several plots and giving page references throughout: “Dadqalato” at pp. 50–53, “Carraweelo iyo Dhuufaaniddii Ragga” at p. 66, Wiil-Waal at pp. 73–89. He notes that the author writes now in the northern and now in the southern dialect of Somali. The review closes with a call for the federal government of Somalia to award Kapchits a Medal of Honour, and with a verse dedication in Somali.

The piece carries an appended list of the author’s earlier books which does not agree at every point with the imprints established here: it gives the publisher of the 2022 collection as “Hill Press” (the book itself reads Hiil Press), dates Four Hyenas to 2015 rather than 2016, and adds a Riga edition of Hubsiimo hal baa la siistaa in 2016 for which no other source has been found. The review also mentions a two-week visit to Somalia in 1992, which does not appear in the documented record of the author’s travels. These points await the author’s confirmation.

Aliqeyr Mohammed Nur on the folktales

“Preserving the Traditional Stories: The Knocked-out Eye of the Hyena”. International Journal of Social Science Exceptional Research, Vol. 4, No. 1, January–February 2025, pp. 75–78. ISSN (online) 2583-8261. Received 22 November 2024, accepted 23 December 2024.

A review article written for the author’s eighty-fifth birthday, by then Research and Development Lead at the Wild Agriculture Archive Bank in Somalia — the same reviewer who wrote on the 2012 proverb collection thirteen years earlier. It places the folktale collection in the sequence of the author’s books, surveys the genres represented in it, and quotes the collection’s own observation that Somali oral prose passes through the hands of narrators who reshape it while keeping its core. The reviewer’s own account of the imprints differs from the one established here — he gives Cairo as the place of publication of the 2022 collection and 2022 as the year of the dictionary, which the author’s information sheet dates to 2020 — and these differences have not been resolved.

Work by colleagues

A. K. Zholkovsky. “The Somali Tale ‘A Soothsayer Tested’”

A condensed English version of the article published in Russian in the journal Narody Azii i Afriki, No. 1. Moscow, 1970. The English version appeared in: Essays in Honour of Muuse Ismaaciil Galaal. Ponte Invisibile, 2011, pp. 53–64.

This is not a review but a scholarly article by Dr. Kapchits’s teacher, devoted to the very tale that gave its title to the 2006 collection Faaliyihii la bilkeyday. Zholkovsky sets the tale apart from the rest of Somali folk prose for its philosophical content and finished form: the narration is interrupted three times by ‘acts’ in verse, which places it midway between poetry and the ordinary folktale. On pain of death a soothsayer must foretell the fate of his tribe; three times a serpent helps him, and three times he treats the serpent in a manner matching what has been predicted — in wartime he raises his sword, in the drought he withholds the promised share, in the year of plenty he brings everything he owns. In the closing monologue the serpent explains that this is not a matter of character: a person’s life echoes the structure of the world.

The analysis proceeds by the method of ‘generative poetics’: the theme is formulated as “revelation: life resembles the structure of the world” (A. Zholkovsky), and the article then shows step by step how the compositional decisions follow from it — the devices of simile and contrastive variation, the anti-theme of a formless, meaningless life, the splitting of the plot into a framing and a framed story, the motifs of contract, order and reward, and finally the sudden turn that makes the closing pronouncement not a moral tacked on to the tale but its denouement.

A. G. Belova. Report on the conference “5000 Years of Semito-Hamitic Languages in Asia and Africa”

Journal Vostok (Oriens), 2011, No. 1, pp. 137–142.

A survey of the international conference held on 22–24 July 2010 at the Free University of Berlin. Dr. Kapchits’s paper on the dialectal division of Somali is mentioned on p. 141 — an independent confirmation of his participation.

Tributes

Khaliil Cabdiraxmaan X. Xasan and six colleagues: “Dr. Georgi Kapchits: Aqoonyahan Abaalmarin Innaga Mudan” (2017)

WardheerNews, 6 August 2017 (© WardheerNews). In Somali.

“A scholar who deserves our award”: the editor of WardheerNews’s interviews recounts how he came to know Kapchits — a message at the end of 2006 from Germany asking the site to bring the newly published Faaliyihii la bilkeyday to Somali readers, the written interview of January 2006 that became one of the site’s most read pieces, and an episode from 2012, when a telephone interview recorded after Kapchits’s return from the Hargeysa book fair had to be partly repeated and the scholar explained that he had not been reading his answers from paper. Khaliil adds that Kapchits once marked every double-letter spelling mistake in an article of his, and that a Russian correcting his Somali taught him to take orthography seriously. The biographical sketch in the piece gives the visits to Somalia as five days in Mogadishu in 1989, a week in Hargeysa in 2001 and a short visit to Djibouti in 2003, with further book-fair visits to Puntland and Somaliland.

The second half gathers six statements. Maxamed Daahir Afrax, chairman of the Regional Academy of the Somali Language in Mogadishu, recalls forty years of correspondence and a recent query about the proverb of the brave man’s three fears, which Kapchits had found quoted at the opening of Hemingway’s “The Short Happy Life of Francis Macomber”, and sets him beside B. W. Andrzejewski (“Macallin Guush”). Siciid Jaamac Xuseen (London) calls him “nin tolnimo sheegtay” — a man who has claimed kinship with the Somali nation. Cabdiraxmaan Faarax Barwaaqo (Hargeysa) dates their contact to the summer of 2002 and the book Magac bilaash uma baxo, their first meeting to Ottawa in 2003, and remembers a shop in San Diego where a Somali shopkeeper was struck dumb by a white man speaking Somali. Idiris Maxamed Cali (Boston) quotes a note in which Kapchits sent greetings to his grandmother. Aadan Makina (Garissa) proposes a state award. C/kariim C/raxmaan X. Xasan (Nairobi) recalls their meeting at the conference on Somali women’s poetry in San Diego in March 2007 and the proverbs with which Kapchits opened the 2006 interview. The piece closes with Gaarriye’s line that a person needs recognition while alive, and with a call for national recognition of Kapchits’s work. The 2012 visit to the Hargeysa book fair, which is not in the documented chronology of the author’s travels, awaits his confirmation.
